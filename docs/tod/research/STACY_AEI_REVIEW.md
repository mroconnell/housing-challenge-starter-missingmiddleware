# The typical scenario's source under review: Stacy et al. (2023) and the AEI critique

Compiled 2026-10-28 at the owner's request ("take the AEI criticism into account"). Our typical scenario, 0.8% of station-area homes over five years, comes from Stacy et al. (2023) (AQ-050).

## The exchange

- **Stacy, Davis, Freemark, Lo, MacDonald, Zheng and Pendall (2023), *Urban Studies*.**
  - Found 180 reforms from newspaper articles, using machine learning plus hand coding, in 1,136 cities across 8 metro areas, 2005–2019: 96 loosened rules and 84 tightened them.
  - Loosening went with a 0.8% rise in housing stock 3–9 years later.
  - Tightening went with no change in supply, but higher rents.
- **Peter, Tracy, Pinto and Baker (AEI Housing Center; working paper October 2025; Econ Journal Watch 23(1), March 2026)** reviewed every article:
  - 60 of the 180 should be excluded: duplicates, the wrong city, or out of scope;
  - 118 of the remaining 120 were misclassified in direction, not citywide, minor, or too vaguely described;
  - only 2 "plausibly qualify as major reforms".
  - Their own long-run case studies (Palisades Park, NJ; Seattle; Denver; over two dozen in all) find that "short and simple, by-right, density-enhancing reforms" sustain 1–2.5% annual growth in housing supply.
- **Stacy, Freemark and Lo, reply (Econ Journal Watch, March 2026):**
  - Most disagreements are about definitions (what counts as major, citywide, or a direction), not facts.
  - The 0.8% "reflects the incremental nature of the reforms enacted during the study period".
  - AEI's larger figures are for larger reforms and are "complementary rather than contradictory".
- **The data:** AEI published Stacy et al.'s full list with its own verdict on each entry (data appendix, January 2026). A derived copy, without article links, is in [reference/stacy_reforms_aei_review.csv](../../../reference/stacy_reforms_aei_review.csv).

## What it means for the TOD scenarios

1. **The typical value probably understates a strong by-right law.** If many of the 96 "loosening" reforms were minor, misplaced or not loosening at all, the 0.8% is the average of a mix of real reforms and noise. Mislabelled reforms pull an average toward zero. The modelled law (SB 79-style, by right, no opt-outs) is the kind AEI calls short and simple.
2. **But our own measurements sit around the same level.** As a share of the whole city's homes over five years:
   - 8 permit-measured cities: 0.04% to 1.24%;
   - 6 census-measured cities: 0.3% to 1.2% (Milpitas 7.7%).

   The typical scenario's 0.8% applies to station-area homes, not the whole city, but neither set of our results points to a much larger typical effect for upzonings of a part of a city.
3. **AEI's 1–2.5% a year** is a whole-city growth rate for selected strong cases, including all building, not only the extra from reform. It is not the same quantity as our "extra homes because of the law", and cannot replace the scenario values directly.
4. **Decision for the owner (AQ-059):** keep the typical value at 0.8% and state the critique on the screens and in the documentation, or re-anchor it, for example on our own measured cities. No number changes without the owner.

## The 84 tightening reforms (downzonings)

- **AEI's verdicts on the 84:**
  - wrong city 19;
  - not major 15;
  - wrong direction 13;
  - one or two neighbourhoods 10;
  - insufficient information 10;
  - duplicates 8;
  - single site 6;
  - other 3.

  None is judged a major citywide downzoning.
- **Stacy's "downzoning had no effect on supply" is therefore weak evidence.** Few of the 84 were real, binding, citywide cuts.
- **Which could feed the up-and-down model:** the 41 left after removing wrong-city, duplicate, wrong-direction and out-of-scope entries. Most are local or minor.
  - Those near rail transit are Pasadena (2016), Culver City (2015), Glendale (2011), Burbank (2017) and Long Beach (2017) in the Los Angeles area; Evanston (2009) and Oak Park (2005) near Chicago; Philadelphia (2006, 2017); and Dallas (2012).
  - Each would need its own boundary map and confirmation from the ordinance, because the source articles are behind a paywall (NewsBank).
