# State transit-density laws: Massachusetts, Utah, Connecticut and an eastern and national sweep

Work package: TOD Gate 1 research, state law classification (east and national sweep).
Prepared: 26 October 2026.

The challenge benchmark, verbatim: "Increase allowable residential density near major transit stops via greater heights and/or floor area ratios. Define 'major transit stop' and justify your proximity threshold. You may assume parking requirements are reduced or eliminated."

How to read this file. Each fact is tagged **verified** when it was read in official statute, regulation, session law or court text (the section is cited), or **source_lead** when it comes from a secondary source (press, advocacy, regional agency or law-firm summary) or from memory and still needs a check against official text. Ratings compare each law with the benchmark:

- **at**: a statewide mandate that raises allowed residential density (height, floor area ratio or units per acre) by right on parcels near defined major transit stops, with no meaningful opt-out.
- **near**: a mandate with a real density floor and by-right approval, but with design features that dilute it (averaging, small required area, partial transit targeting, long phase-in).
- **weaker**: planning-only, opt-in or incentive-only, affordability-conditioned, parking-only or mixed-use-only, or otherwise not a guaranteed density increase near transit.

West Coast and Mountain West laws (California SB 79 and earlier, Oregon, Washington HB 1491, Colorado HB24-1313) are outside this file's scope and are expected in the companion western file. They are mentioned only where they help calibrate a rating.

Headline finding: no eastern or national-sweep state has enacted a law that fully meets the benchmark. Massachusetts is the only enacted, enforced mandate with a numeric by-right density floor tied (partly) to transit, and its own monitoring shows weak transit targeting. Every other enacted law is planning-only, opt-in, affordability-conditioned, mixed-use-only or parking-only. The one bill that would have met the benchmark outright (Virginia SB 717) stalled.

## 1. Massachusetts: MBTA Communities, M.G.L. c.40A §3A

### Citation and dates

- Statute: M.G.L. c.40A §3A, "Multi-family zoning as-of-right in MBTA communities." Official text: https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleVII/Chapter40A/Section3A. **verified** (text read in full).
- Enactment: added by the 2020 economic development act (St. 2020, c.358), signed by Governor Baker in January 2021. The Attorney General's January 2026 complaint states the law "was signed into law by Governor Charlie Baker in January 2021." Session-law chapter number and exact signing date (14 January 2021): **source_lead**.
- Effective date of the statute: on enactment, January 2021 (**source_lead**). In practice nothing was required of towns until state guidance existed.
- Guidance history: draft guidelines 2021; "Compliance Guidelines for Multi-family Zoning Districts" issued 10 August 2022 and revised 17 August 2023 (**verified**, 760 CMR 72.04(1)(b)2 and the Attorney General's complaint ¶19). The Supreme Judicial Court held on 8 January 2025 that those guidelines were unenforceable because they were not promulgated under the Administrative Procedure Act (**verified**, Attorney General v. Town of Milton, SJC-13580, 495 Mass. 183 (2025)). Emergency regulations 760 CMR 72.00 took effect 14 January 2025 and final regulations on 11 April 2025 (**verified**, 760 CMR 72.01 and Mass. Register notes).
- Local compliance deadlines (760 CMR 72.09(3), Table 3, **verified**): rapid transit communities 31 December 2023; commuter rail communities 31 December 2024; adjacent communities 31 December 2024; adjacent small towns 31 December 2025; any rapid transit, commuter rail or adjacent community that had not applied by its original deadline got a reset deadline of 14 July 2025. So local compliance came between about 3 years and 5 years after enactment.

### What the law requires

- Mandate: each of the 177 MBTA communities "shall have a zoning ordinance or by-law that provides for at least 1 district of reasonable size in which multi-family housing is permitted as of right," without age restrictions and suitable for families with children (§3A(a)(1), **verified**).
- Density floor: "a minimum gross density of 15 units per acre," subject to wetlands and septic (Title 5) limits (§3A(a)(1)(i), **verified**). The regulations allow sub-districts with different densities as long as the district as a whole averages at least 15 units per acre (760 CMR 72.06(3), **verified**). This is an "on average" capacity rule, not a per-parcel right.
- Stop definition: "commuter rail station, subway station, ferry terminal or bus station" (§3A(a)(1)(ii), **verified**). The regulations define "bus station" narrowly as Silver Line platforms, plus other high-capacity bus locations the state designates on request (760 CMR 72.02, **verified**). Commuter rail stations must have year-round service (760 CMR 72.02, **verified**).
- Distance: "not more than 0.5 miles from" a station, "if applicable" (§3A(a)(1)(ii), **verified**). "Transit Station Area" is land within 0.5 miles of a station (760 CMR 72.02, **verified**). The rule is "applicable" only to a town with at least 100 acres of developable station area; a town with less may put the district anywhere (760 CMR 72.08(1)(a) and (d), **verified**). Where it applies, the share of the district's minimum land area and minimum unit capacity that must sit in station areas rises from 20 percent (101 to 250 developable station acres) to 90 percent (801 or more acres) (760 CMR 72.08, Table 2, **verified**).
- District size: minimum land area of 50 acres or 1.5 percent of developable land, whichever is less, for rapid transit, commuter rail and adjacent communities; no minimum for adjacent small towns; at least half the district must be contiguous and pieces under 5 acres do not count (760 CMR 72.05(1)(a), **verified**). Minimum unit capacity equals 25 percent (rapid transit), 15 percent (commuter rail), 10 percent (adjacent) or 5 percent (adjacent small town) of the town's existing housing units, with a floor of minimum acres times 15 units per acre and a cap of 25 percent of existing units (760 CMR 72.05(1)(b), **verified**). Capacity is measured with the state's compliance model spreadsheet (760 CMR 72.05(1)(d), **verified**). Mixed-use districts can offset up to 25 percent of required capacity (760 CMR 72.05(1)(c), **verified**).
- By right: "As of right" means no special permit, variance, zoning amendment, waiver or other discretionary approval (760 CMR 72.02 and 72.04(1), **verified**). Site plan review is allowed but may not "unreasonably delay a project nor impose conditions that make it infeasible" (760 CMR 72.04(1)(a), **verified**).
- Affordability cap: an inclusionary requirement is consistent with as-of-right zoning if no more than 10 percent of units must be affordable at no deeper than 80 percent of area median income; the state may approve up to 20 percent with an independent economic feasibility analysis, or in a Chapter 40R district; never above 20 percent except pre-existing 40R districts at 25 percent (760 CMR 72.04(1)(b), **verified**).
- Parking: the statute is silent; parking minimums are an input to the compliance model, so heavy parking requirements reduce counted capacity, but there is no parking cap (760 CMR 72.05(1)(d) and 72.06(2)(a), **verified**).
- Enforcement: a noncompliant town loses eligibility for the Housing Choice Initiative, Local Capital Projects Fund, MassWorks and HousingWorks (§3A(b), **verified**), and compliance is weighed in 13 other discretionary grant programs (760 CMR 72.09(1), **verified**). The Supreme Judicial Court held the duty is mandatory and the Attorney General may sue for declaratory and injunctive relief (Milton, 495 Mass. 183, **verified**). On 29 January 2026 the Attorney General sued 9 towns (Dracut, East Bridgewater, Halifax, Holden, Marblehead, Middleton, Tewksbury, Wilmington, Winthrop) (**verified**, mass.gov press release and complaint). As of May 2026, Marblehead had adopted a plan and 8 of the 9 remained noncompliant; A Better City describes Marblehead's district as "paper compliance" because more than 70 percent of it is a privately owned country club (**source_lead**, A Better City, 18 May 2026).

### Production and monitoring data

Boston Indicators, "An Early Look at the MBTA Communities Permitting Pipeline," Amy Dain, January 2026 (report PDF dated 27 January 2026). All figures are from the report text (**verified** against the report PDF; the report itself relies on the state's EOHLC project tracker, which it calls crowd-sourced and likely incomplete):

- Pipeline size: 102 projects containing 6,898 housing units (6,804 net new units) in 34 municipalities, as of 12 January 2026. Projects are at permitting, construction or occupancy stages, most still in permitting.
- Scale against stock: if all 6,898 units were built, they would be "substantially less than a 1 percent increase" on the nearly 2,000,000 homes in the 177 MBTA communities.
- Project sizes: 58 projects with fewer than 30 units (443 units, 6 percent of pipeline units); 24 projects with 30 to 99 units (1,135 units, 16 percent); 19 projects with 100 or more units (5,290 units, 77 percent). The 83 projects under 100 units total 1,608 units, an average of 19 units per project.
- Transit share: about 60 percent of projects and 30 percent of units are within 0.5 mile of a train station, measured as the crow flies; about 57 percent of units are within 1 mile. Dain concludes that "as a tool for directing development specifically to the half-mile walksheds of train stations, the law has shown weak results."
- Why: 96 of the 177 communities (82 with no developable station land plus 14 with under 100 developable station acres) were allowed to put the district anywhere; only 10 communities faced the 90 percent in-station requirement. The Boston Foundation's 2025 report card found 62 percent of zoned capacity within 0.5 mile across 104 communities analysed, which Dain says over-represents station towns.
- Land: districts adopted in 141 cities and towns cover slightly more than 1 percent of their combined land area (statistic from Maxwell Palmer, cited in the report).
- Compliance: 165 of 177 communities had submitted compliance applications by the end of 2025; 126 determinations of compliance and 7 of conditional compliance had been issued; 12 communities were noncompliant.
- Concentration: Lexington alone has 1,286 units in 12 projects, nearly one-fifth of the pipeline, and only 4 of those 12 projects are under construction.

The state's own tracker: EOHLC keeps the project list used by Dain (on the mass.gov "Multi-family zoning requirement for MBTA Communities" page). The mass.gov site returned HTTP 403 to automated fetches during this research, so the live tracker count after January 2026 was not read (**source_lead**). The Attorney General's January 2026 release repeats "nearly 7,000 more homes across 34 communities" (**verified**).

### Rating: near

Massachusetts is the only enacted, actively enforced state mandate in this sweep with a numeric by-right density floor and an explicit half-mile transit radius, and the courts and Attorney General have closed the "voluntary" loophole. But the floor is an average of 15 units per acre across a district that can be as small as 50 acres, the half-mile rule bites only in towns with at least 100 developable station acres and then only for 20 to 90 percent of the district, inclusionary requirements up to 10 to 20 percent are allowed, and local compliance arrived 3 to 5 years after enactment. The measured result, about 6,900 pipeline units in 5 years with only 30 percent within half a mile of a station, shows that the poison pills the owner listed (capacity "on average", small required areas, partial transit targeting, long delays) do bind. For a Wisconsin model, Massachusetts is the best US analog for the mechanism of a statewide mandate, but its transit dosage should be read as diluted.

## 2. Utah: station area plans (HB 462, 2022) and related tools

### Citation and dates

- Original section: Utah Code 10-9a-403.1, "Station area plan requirements," enacted by H.B. 462 (2022), "Housing Affordability Amendments" (**verified** that HB 462 amended 10-9a and 72-2-124, via le.utah.gov bill page "Sections Affected"). Effective date of 2022 general session bills: 4 May 2022 (**source_lead**).
- Renumbering: the 2025 recodification moved 10-9a-403.1 to 10-21-203 (**source_lead**, Utah League of Cities and Towns crosswalk). In the 2026 general session, S.B. 39 renumbers 10-21-203 to 63N-23-104 effective 6 May 2026, while H.B. 68 amends 10-21-203 effective 1 July 2026 (**verified**, enrolled S.B. 39 Section 30 and enrolled H.B. 68 Section 3 and following). The Office of Legislative Research will reconcile the two; the substance is unchanged. Official enrolled bills: https://le.utah.gov/Session/2026/bills/enrolled/SB0039.pdf and https://le.utah.gov/Session/2026/bills/enrolled/HB0068.pdf. The le.utah.gov section pages load by script and could not be read directly.
- Local deadlines (**verified**, S.B. 39 text of 63N-23-104 and H.B. 68 text of 10-21-203): for existing stations, a municipality must adopt a plan and "appropriate" implementing land use regulations on or before 31 December 2025; a city with more than 4 existing stations must finish 4 by that date and at least 2 more each year after; a new station must have a plan before service starts; a private "qualifying land use petition" can force a plan within 12 months; one 12-month extension is available with the metropolitan planning organization's sign-off.

### What the law requires

- Stop definition and distance: "station area" means the area within a 0.5 mile radius of the platform center for a fixed-guideway station that provides rail service, or 0.25 mile for a fixed-guideway station that provides bus service only (bus rapid transit); any parcel bisected by the radius is included (**verified**, S.B. 39 line 422 and following definitions).
- Content: the plan must "promote" four objectives, the first being "increasing the availability and affordability of housing, including moderate income housing" (**verified**, subsection (6)(a)(i) in the 2026 text). The plan may cover only part of the station area (**verified**, subsection (7)(b)(ii)).
- Density floor: none. There is no units-per-acre, height or floor-area minimum (**verified**, no such term in the section).
- By right: no. The city adopts its own vision and zoning; plans are certified by the Wasatch Front Regional Council or Mountainland Association of Governments (**verified**).
- Escape valves: a city can satisfy the requirement by resolution that pre-June 2022 plans "substantially promote" the objectives, or by resolution that conditions (existing development, entitlements, ownership, environmental limits, "market readiness") make a plan "impracticable" (**verified**, subsection (1)(b)).
- Affordability: no mandate inside the station plan itself.
- Parking: no statutory parking change.
- Enforcement: a municipality with a fixed-guideway station must include the station-area-plan strategy in its moderate income housing plan (**verified**, H.B. 68, 10-21-201(2)(d) and (3)(a)(iii)(U)). Failure to file a compliant moderate income housing report makes the city ineligible for Transportation Investment Fund and Transit Transportation Investment Fund programming and triggers fees of $250 per day (and $500 per day in a consecutive year) payable to the Olene Walker Housing Loan Fund (**verified**, H.B. 68, 10-21-202(8)(b)). Cities must report on progress to the planning organization every 5 years (**verified**).

### Related Utah tool: Housing and Transit Reinvestment Zones

Utah also lets a city voluntarily create a Housing and Transit Reinvestment Zone around a commuter rail station, bus rapid transit station or transit hub to capture tax increment. A zone must devote at least 51 percent of developable area to residential use at an average of at least 50 dwelling units per acre (39 units per acre for bus rapid transit stations and county transit hubs) and make at least 12 percent of units affordable (**verified**, S.B. 39 text renumbering 63N-3-603 and following). This is a strong density target, but it applies only where a city opts in, so it does not meet the benchmark as a statewide rule.

### Monitoring data

- Wasatch Front Regional Council, 23 October 2025: 72 certified station area plans that "pave the way for more than 75,000 new homes near transit" (**source_lead**; this is planned capacity, not permits).
- WFRC progress update, September 2025: 95 eligible stations along the Wasatch Front, 127 station areas when split by jurisdiction; 83 of 127 station areas had received technical assistance; $3.972 million allocated (**source_lead**).
- No study of permits attributable to station area plans was found.

### Rating: weaker

Utah's law defines major stops clearly (rail and bus rapid transit, 0.5 mile and 0.25 mile) and has real money penalties, but it mandates a plan, not a density outcome. There is no density or height floor, no by-right entitlement, the plan need not cover the whole station area, and a city can satisfy the law with an "impracticability" resolution. It is a useful template for stop definitions and certification, but should not be modelled as a zoning capacity increase.

## 3. Connecticut: PA 25-1 (November 2025 special session, HB 8002)

### Citation and dates

- Public Act No. 25-1, November Special Session 2025, "An Act Concerning Housing Growth" (HB 8002). Official text: https://www.cga.ct.gov/2025/ACT/PA/PDF/2025PA-00001-R00HB-08002SS1-PA.PDF. Approved by the governor 26 November 2025 (**verified**, "Governor's Action" line).
- Transit-oriented district provisions: §11, effective 1 January 2026 (**verified**). Housing growth infrastructure grant program to be established by 1 July 2028 (§15, **verified**). Parking reform §19 effective 1 July 2026 (**verified**). Middle housing on commercial lots §16 effective 1 July 2026 (**verified**).

### What the law does

- Opt-in: a "qualifying transit-oriented community" is a town that adopts a transit-oriented district of reasonable size (as judged by the Office of Policy and Management) containing a rapid transit station or regular bus station, or a town bordering one that designates a district in or next to its downtown (§11(a)(6) to (8), **verified**). Nothing requires a town to do so; §11(h) says the state may not deem a town qualifying "without the approval of such municipality" (**verified**).
- Stop definition: "rapid transit station" means any station serving any rail or rapid bus route; "regular bus service station" means a fixed stop with service at least once every 60 minutes in peak hours on a fixed route (§11(a)(9) and (10), **verified**).
- Distance: the district must include land within a 0.5 mile radius of the station, or be within a "reasonable distance" (set by the secretary) of other transit, a commercial corridor or downtown (§11(a)(6)(A) and (B), **verified**).
- Density floor: none in units per acre, height or floor area. Density, height and floor area ratio appear only as topics for future state guidelines (§11(j)(1), **verified**).
- By right inside a district: (A) 2 to 9 unit "transit community middle housing"; (B) projects of 10 or more units if at least 30 percent are set-aside units under §8-30g; (C) fully deed-restricted (40 years, 60 percent of median income) projects on municipal, housing authority, nonprofit or religious land; plus conversions to those types (§11(e), **verified**). Market-rate projects of 10 or more units are not by right and, if approved, must carry a 5 or 10 percent deed restriction depending on market type (§11(f) and (g), **verified**).
- Escape valves: a qualifying town can seek an exemption from the as-of-right rules and the guidelines; if denied it may "opt out" and return any funding (§11(j)(2) and (3), **verified**).
- Incentive: eligibility for the housing growth program grants (§11(b) and §15, **verified**).
- Statewide parking reform (applies everywhere, not only near transit): a town may not reject a residential project solely for failing parking minimums, except it may require parking for projects of more than 16 units subject to a parking needs assessment cap of 1 space per studio or 1-bedroom and 2 per larger unit; towns may designate up to 2 "conservation and traffic mitigation districts" of up to 4 percent of land area where small projects can face minimums (§19 and the amended §8-2 list, **verified**).
- Middle housing statewide: from 1 July 2026, zoning must allow 2 to 9 unit buildings or mixed use on any lot zoned commercial or mixed use, by "summary review" (§16, **verified**). This is not transit-specific.

### Vetoed 2025 bill (note only)

HB 5002 (2025 regular session), a broader housing bill that included the "Work, Live, Ride" transit-oriented framework, "fair share" allocations and wider parking repeal, passed both chambers and was vetoed by Governor Lamont in June 2025 (**source_lead**, Greenwich Free Press and CT Mirror, November 2025). HB 8002 was the negotiated replacement.

### Monitoring data

None yet. The grant program and reporting start by 1 July 2028 (§15(f), **verified**).

### Rating: weaker

Connecticut defines stops (rail and rapid bus, and hourly bus) and uses a half-mile radius, but the whole framework is opt-in with an explicit exit, sets no density, height or floor-area floor, and grants by-right approval only to small middle housing or projects carrying 30 to 100 percent affordable units. Market-rate apartment buildings near stations still need discretionary approval plus a deed restriction. It is an incentive program, not a density mandate.

## 4. Sweep of other states, 2019 to 2026

### Maryland (two enacted laws, both weaker)

**HB 538 (2024), Housing Expansion and Affordability Act; Land Use Article §7-503.** Official text: https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/laws/StatuteText?article=glu&section=7-503 (**verified**).
- Stops and distance: present or planned MARC, Baltimore Metro, Light Rail, Metrorail or other passenger rail stations in Maryland; property wholly within 0.75 mile (§7-503(a)(2)(ii) and (3), **verified**).
- Density: a "qualified project" gets 30 percent more density than otherwise allowed in multifamily and mixed-use zones; up to the highest multifamily density in nonresidential zones; middle housing in single-family zones (§7-503(c), **verified**). But areas zoned single-family on 1 January 2024 are exempt from the bonus (§7-503(b)(2), **verified**).
- Affordability condition: at least 15 percent of units deed-restricted affordable for 40 years, or 20 percent where local rules already meet that bar (§7-503(a)(2)(iii) and (iv), **verified**); state guidance says only Montgomery County is at 20 percent (**source_lead**, DHCD HB 538 FAQ, updated 18 May 2026).
- Dates: enacted in the 2024 session; effective date and chapter number not checked (**source_lead**).
- Rating: **weaker**. A bonus that requires a 15 to 20 percent, 40-year affordability set-aside is exactly the "affordability mandate that can make projects infeasible" pattern, and single-family land, which covers most station areas outside the urban cores, is excluded.

**HB 894 (2026), Chapter 592, Maryland Transit and Housing Opportunity Act.** Chapter text: https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/Chapters_noln/CH_592_hb0894e.pdf. Approved 26 May 2026; effective 1 October 2026 (**verified**, Section 6 and approval line).
- Stops: rail transit stations with at least hourly service on average from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. Monday to Friday (**verified**, new Land Use §4-104(e) and (f)). MDOT lists WMATA rail, Purple Line, Baltimore Light Rail and Metro, and the MARC Penn Line from New Carrollton to Baltimore Penn Station (**source_lead**, MDOT page).
- Land use: local zoning "shall allow mixed-use development on land designated for residential use or appropriate commercial use" within 0.5 mile (**verified**, §4-104(e)). For state, Baltimore City or WMATA land in transportation use contiguous to such a station, under an MDOT-approved TOD plan, a local body "may not impose limitations or restrictions on land use classification, height, or setback" (**verified**, §4-104(f)).
- Parking: no minimum off-street parking for residential or mixed-use projects within 0.25 mile, unless an adequate parking study is approved (**verified**, §4-104(c)(2)).
- Exclusions: campus stations, straddling parcels, and any area zoned single-family on 1 January 2026 (**verified**, §4-104(b)(2)).
- No density, height or floor-area floor on private land. The state estimates more than 300 acres of state land could support about 7,000 units (**source_lead**, governor's and DHCD releases, 26 May 2026).
- Rating: **weaker**. It is a genuine state preemption on state-owned station land and a parking repeal, but on private land it only requires that mixed use be allowed, with no height or density increase, and it excludes single-family-zoned land.

Earlier Maryland TOD legislation (2023 bills on TOD designation and financing) was about state designation and finance, not zoning capacity (**source_lead**; not separately read).

### Rhode Island (weaker)

R.I. Gen. Laws §45-24-77, Transit-oriented development pilot program, enacted 2023 (P.L. 2023, ch. 323 and 324, effective 1 January 2024) and amended 2025 (P.L. 2025, ch. 258 and 289, effective 27 June 2025). It lets developers or towns apply for funds for projects within 0.25 mile of a regional mobility hub or 0.125 mile of a frequent transit stop, provided the town has a certified high-density zoning district (**source_lead**, statute text via a legal database mirror; official rilegislature.gov text not read). Rules were adopted 12 February 2026, and the state housing office says the program "is not currently funded" (**source_lead**, housing.ri.gov). Rating: **weaker** (voluntary, unfunded incentive). Rhode Island's 2025 H. 5800 allows housing in commercial zones and is not transit-specific (**source_lead**, Pew, September 2025).

### New Jersey (nothing enacted)

The Transit Village Initiative (37 designated municipalities) is a voluntary designation with small grants, $1.7 million in fiscal year 2026 (**source_lead**, NJDOT, 15 December 2025). The 2024 to 2025 parking bill S2974 (would have eliminated parking minimums within 0.5 mile of rail) did not become law and was reintroduced as S3718 in 2026; A945 (2026) would create an "Enhanced Transit Village Program" with 50 and 25 units per acre as-of-right options, and is only introduced (**source_lead**, njleg.gov bill pages). No enacted statewide transit-density law found.

### New York (nothing enacted)

Governor Hochul's 2023 Housing Compact proposed requiring rezoning within 0.5 mile of MTA rail stations, but it was dropped from the 2023 budget (**source_lead**). A5137 (25 units per acre within 0.5 mile of covered transit facilities) has sat in the Assembly Local Governments Committee since 2025 (**source_lead**, nyassembly.gov). The fiscal year 2027 budget's "Let Them Build" package (signed 27 May 2026) creates environmental review (SEQRA) exemptions for housing up to 100 to 500 units by area type but states it "does not supersede ... local zoning" (**source_lead**, governor's release). No transit-density mandate enacted.

### Virginia (parking only enacted; density bill stalled)

- HB 888 (2026), signed 22 April 2026, effective 1 July 2026, caps minimum parking at 0.5 space per unit for multifamily or mixed use within 0.5 mile of mass transit (**source_lead**, McGuireWoods alert, June 2026). Parking only: **weaker**.
- SB 717 (2026) would have required overlay districts allowing 6 stories and 30 units per acre within 0.25 mile of heavy, light or commuter rail and bus rapid transit, and 2 to 4 units per lot to 1 mile, approved ministerially. It passed the Senate 21 to 19 on 5 February 2026 and was continued to 2027 by the House Counties, Cities and Towns Committee on 20 February 2026 (**source_lead**, LegiScan and VACo). As drafted it would have rated **at**.
- SB 74 / HB 867 (2026, Chapter 436) broaden affordable dwelling unit program authority and "encourage" transit-oriented development; not a density mandate (**source_lead**).

### Illinois (parking only)

SB 2111 (2025), the Northern Illinois Transit Act, passed 31 October 2025 and includes a ban on parking minimums within 0.5 mile of a transit hub or 0.125 mile of a transit corridor, effective 1 June 2026, applying to home-rule municipalities including Chicago, plus powers for the new transit authority to develop land near stations (**source_lead**, Streetsblog Chicago, Capitol News Illinois, Furman Center tracker). Rating: **weaker** (parking only, no density change).

### Hawaii (incentive only)

Act 159 (2025), HB 1409, signed 3 June 2025, creates a Mixed-Income Subaccount in the Rental Housing Revolving Fund that prioritizes projects with "transit-supportive density," asks the TOD infrastructure board to consider those needs, and asks the state TOD council to delineate county TOD areas (**source_lead**, LegiScan and the signed act posted by the Hawaii Office of Planning). This confirms the earlier belief: it is a funding and planning law, not a density mandate. Rating: **weaker**.

### Minnesota (nothing enacted)

No transit-density law enacted in 2023 to 2026. The 2026 bill H.F. 4449 (transit-oriented zoning conditions on transit capital spending in the Twin Cities) was only introduced, with key parameters "to be determined" (**source_lead**, House Research summary, 10 April 2026).

### Maine (not a transit law)

LD 1829 (2025, Public Law 2025 chapter 385, approved 20 June 2025) raises the affordable housing density bonus to 2.5 times base density and adds a height allowance for affordable projects in growth areas; state guidance says LD 1829, LD 997 and LD 427 "do not require municipalities to take transit into consideration" (**source_lead**, Maine legislature chaptered text and Maine Office of Community Affairs FAQ). Not a transit-density law.

### Montana, New Hampshire, Arizona, Texas (confirmed not transit-density laws)

- Montana SB 243 (2025) allows apartments up to 60 feet in commercial and industrial zones; SB 245 (2023) allows multifamily and mixed use in commercial zones in larger cities. Neither is tied to transit (**source_lead**, Pew 2025; SB 245 details from memory).
- New Hampshire HB 631 (2025) allows residential development in commercial zones; not transit-tied (**source_lead**, Pew).
- Arizona HB 2110 (2025) requires large cities to allow multifamily on 10 percent of commercial and mixed-use land; not transit-tied (**source_lead**, Pew).
- Texas SB 840 (2025) allows apartments in commercial zones in large and midsize cities; SB 2477 eases office conversions. Not transit-tied (**source_lead**, Pew).

These confirm the earlier belief that Montana SB 243 and SB 245, Hawaii Act 159, and the 2025 Arizona, Texas and New Hampshire laws are not transit-density laws. They are commercial-corridor or general by-right laws and belong in a different benchmark category.

## 5. Implications for the Wisconsin TOD classification

1. Among eastern and national-sweep states, only Massachusetts provides an enacted, enforced, numerically specified, by-right mandate that is partly transit-targeted. Treat it as "near", and treat its measured transit share (30 percent of pipeline units within 0.5 mile) as evidence that averaging and local siting choice dilute transit dosage.
2. The poison-pill patterns the owner listed all appear in enacted laws: opt-outs (Connecticut, Rhode Island, Utah's impracticability resolution), affordability conditions (Maryland HB 538 at 15 to 20 percent for 40 years; Connecticut at 30 percent for 10 or more units by right), capacity "on average" (Massachusetts), small required areas (Massachusetts 50 acres, about 1 percent of land), exclusion of single-family zones (both Maryland laws), and multi-year compliance delays (Massachusetts 3 to 5 years; Utah 3.5 years to the first deadline).
3. Several states reached the benchmark's optional parking assumption without raising density (Illinois, Virginia HB 888, Maryland HB 894 within 0.25 mile, Connecticut statewide). These are parking-only laws and should not be scored as TOD density laws.
4. The only eastern bill drafted at the benchmark (Virginia SB 717: 6 stories and 30 units per acre within 0.25 mile, ministerial approval) did not pass, which suggests that a Wisconsin law modelled "at" the benchmark has no enacted eastern precedent and must borrow its parameters from western laws (California SB 79, Washington HB 1491, Colorado HB24-1313), to be documented in the western file.
