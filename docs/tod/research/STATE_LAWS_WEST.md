# State transit-oriented density laws: West Coast, Colorado and British Columbia

Research work package for the Wisconsin transit-oriented density (TOD) reform. Compiled 26 October 2026.

The benchmark we rate against, verbatim: "Increase allowable residential density near major transit stops via greater heights and/or floor area ratios. Define 'major transit stop' and justify your proximity threshold. You may assume parking requirements are reduced or eliminated."

Ratings:

- **at**: the law gives parcel-level height or floor area ratio (FAR) rights near defined transit stops, approval is by right or ministerial, and opt-outs, delays and affordability conditions are small enough that most station-area parcels gain real capacity within a few years.
- **near**: the core mechanism matches the benchmark, but one or two significant escape hatches (averaging, multi-year delays, exclusion maps, mandatory affordability set-asides, rezoning still required) materially shrink or postpone the effect.
- **weaker**: the law sets a jurisdiction-wide capacity target, a planning duty or a grant incentive rather than parcel-level height or FAR rights at defined transit stops.

Evidence tags: **verified** means read in official statute, enrolled bill or rule text (section given). **source_lead** means taken from a secondary source (named) or an agency web page summary, and should be checked before it is relied on.

Bottom line for Wisconsin: the two designs closest to the benchmark wording are California SB 79's default standards (parcel-level height, density and FAR by tier, ministerial path) and British Columbia's prescribed FAR and storey floors. Washington is the strongest on parking and has an automatic state fallback, but uses averages and a heavy affordability mandate. Colorado and Oregon are capacity-planning laws and should be treated as a different (weaker) class.

## 1. California

### 1.1 SB 79 (2025), "Abundant and Affordable Homes Near Transit Act"

**Citation and URL.** Senate Bill 79, Chapter 512, Statutes of 2025, adding Government Code Chapter 4.1.5, sections 65912.155 to 65912.162. Official text: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB79 (verified).

**Dates.**
- Enacted: approved by the Governor and filed with the Secretary of State on 10 October 2025 (verified, chaptered text header).
- Effective: 1 January 2026 as an ordinary statute, but the upzoning section is not operative for a local agency until 1 July 2026, unless the agency adopts an HCD-compliant ordinance or alternative plan earlier (verified, 65912.157(n)).
- Unincorporated county areas: not until the 7th regional housing needs allocation cycle (verified, 65912.157(n)).
- Housing Accountability Act penalty presumption for denials in high-resource areas: from 1 January 2027 (verified, 65912.157(m)).
- Temporary exclusions: available until 1 year after adoption of the 7th housing element revision (verified, 65912.161(b)(1)). For the large Bay Area and Southern California regions this is roughly 2030 to 2031 (source_lead, inferred from the 7th-cycle schedule; Santa Monica reported its exclusion runs "until 2030", Westside Current, 31 August 2026).
- 2026 amendment: AB 2576 (Chapter 214, Statutes of 2026, approved 14 September 2026) adds state-inventory historic districts and listed historic parcels designated before 1 January 2025 to the exclusions in 65912.161 (source_lead, GovBuddy bill tracker; not yet read on leginfo). AB 2415 also amends 65912.161 (source_lead, same).

**Station definition.** A "transit-oriented development stop" is a major transit stop under Public Resources Code 21064.3 (plus planned stops with a selected preferred alternative or in a regional transportation improvement program) served by heavy rail, very high frequency commuter rail (72 or more trains per day, both directions), high frequency commuter rail (48 or more trains per day), light rail (including streetcar), or, within an urban transit county, bus service meeting PRC 21060.2(a)(1) (verified, 65912.156(e), (p), (r)). PRC 21060.2(a)(1) is the dedicated-bus-lane bus rapid transit standard (source_lead: we could not load 21060.2 itself; San Diego Ordinance O-22099 recital C describes it as bus stops "located on a full-time dedicated bus lane or separate public right-of-way"). An "urban transit county" is a county with more than 15 passenger rail stations (verified, 65912.156(q)). HCD's advisory memo to the metropolitan planning organizations lists 7 such counties as of July 2026: Alameda, Los Angeles, Sacramento, San Francisco, San Mateo, Santa Clara and San Diego (verified, HCD "SB 79 Advisory Clarifications on Definitions", hosted by MTC). Some law-firm summaries list 8 counties including Orange (source_lead, Sheppard Mullin); the HCD list should govern.
- Tier 1 stop: heavy rail or very high frequency commuter rail (verified, 65912.156(n)).
- Tier 2 stop: light rail, high frequency commuter rail, or qualifying bus (verified, 65912.156(o)).
- New routes not in a regional transportation plan by 1 January 2026 count only if Tier 1; bus stops in counties that become urban transit counties after 1 July 2026 never count (verified, 65912.156(p)).

**Distance tiers.** Within 0.25 mile and from 0.25 to 0.5 mile, measured in a straight line from the nearest parcel edge to a pedestrian access point (verified, 65912.157(a), (b)). "Adjacent" means within 200 feet of a pedestrian access point (verified, 65912.156(a)). The outer ring (0.25 to 0.5 mile) applies only in cities with at least 35,000 residents (verified, 65912.157(a)(4) and (a)(6)).

**Density, height and FAR floors** (local government may not impose limits below these; verified, 65912.157(a)(3) to (a)(6)):

| Location | Height (feet) | Maximum density may not be set below (dwelling units per acre) | Residential FAR that may not be physically precluded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1, within 0.25 mile | 75 | 120 | 3.5 |
| Tier 1, 0.25 to 0.5 mile (cities of 35,000 or more) | 65 | 100 | 3.0 |
| Tier 2, within 0.25 mile | 65 | 100 | 3.0 |
| Tier 2, 0.25 to 0.5 mile (cities of 35,000 or more) | 55 | 80 | 2.5 |

- Adjacency intensifier: projects adjacent to a stop get an extra 20 feet of height, 40 dwelling units per acre and 1.0 of FAR before the density bonus (verified, 65912.157(e)).
- Project eligibility: at least 5 units, at least 30 dwelling units per acre (or the local minimum if higher), and an average unit size of no more than 1,750 net habitable square feet (verified, 65912.157(a)(1), (a)(2)).
- The site must already be zoned residential, mixed or commercial; single-family residential zones count as "residential" (verified, 65912.157(a)). Projects stack with the state density bonus using SB 79 density as the base (verified, 65912.157(d)).

**By-right versus discretionary.** Housing is an "allowed use" (verified, 65912.157(a)). Projects are eligible for streamlined ministerial approval under Gov. Code 65913.4 (the SB 35/SB 423 process), but must then meet that section's other conditions, including its affordability levels and the very high fire hazard zone bar (verified, 65912.159(a)). Projects not using 65913.4 go through normal local review, but conflicting local standards do not apply and the Housing Accountability Act protects them (verified, 65912.159(b), 65912.157(l)). Labor standards apply to buildings over 85 feet (verified, 65912.157(k)). Local demolition and anti-displacement ordinances apply, and sites with rent-controlled tenancies in the past 7 years are excluded (verified, 65912.157(g), (h)).

**Opt-outs, alternative compliance and exemptions.**
- Local alternative plan: must keep total net zoned capacity (units and floor area) across all station zones, may cut any one site's density by up to 50 percent, may not cut any one station zone's capacity by more than 50 percent, and Tier 2 sites may not drop below 30 units per acre and FAR 1.0 (verified, 65912.161(a)). Once HCD approves, SB 79 does not apply in that jurisdiction until the next housing element amendment (verified, 65912.161(d)). This is the "average rather than parcel" escape hatch.
- Temporary exclusions until 1 year after the 7th housing element: sites already zoned at 50 percent or more of the SB 79 standards; whole station zones where 33 percent of sites meet 50 percent and the zone holds 75 percent of aggregate SB 79 density; low-resource station zones holding 40 percent; low-resource sites in a city holding 50 percent of total SB 79 capacity; sites under an alternative plan; very high fire hazard or state responsibility areas; sea-level-rise sites; and locally registered historic sites designated as of 1 January 2025 (verified, 65912.161(b)(1)).
- Permanent exemptions by ordinance: areas with no walking path of less than 1 mile to the stop, and industrial employment hubs of at least 250 contiguous acres in cities with at least 15 stops (verified, 65912.160(e)).
- Exclusion maps: the local zoning map must show which sites are and are not covered (verified, 65912.161(b)(2)).
- HCD review: draft ordinance 14 days before adoption, final within 60 days, HCD finding within 90 days plus an optional 30 days; silence deems the ordinance compliant for penalty purposes (verified, 65912.160(d)).

**Affordability.** Projects over 10 units must reserve 7 percent of units for extremely low income, or 10 percent for very low income, or 13 percent for lower income households, for 55 years (rental) or 45 years (ownership); a stricter local inclusionary rule replaces these (verified, 65912.157(i)). Cities may keep inclusionary rules that do not, alone or together, prevent the standards (verified, 65912.157(c)).

**Parking.** SB 79 has no parking section of its own; projects use density bonus parking ratios (verified, 65912.157(d)) and AB 2097 (see 1.3).

**Parcel-level or average.** Parcel-level by default. Becomes jurisdiction-average only where an HCD-approved alternative plan is in force.

**Monitoring and early data.**
- HCD oversees compliance and posts review letters (verified, HCD SB 79 page, https://www.hcd.ca.gov/planning-and-research/sb79-tod). HCD found San Francisco's alternative plan and exclusion ordinance (Ordinance 082-26) substantially compliant on 1 July 2026, on condition that the city not enforce a ban on density bonus use (verified, HCD review letter). HCD gave Los Angeles technical assistance on 22 June 2026 on an ordinance to exempt and temporarily exclude sites (verified, HCD letter).
- Los Angeles adopted a "Phased Implementation Ordinance" and "Low-Rise Ordinance" effective 30 June 2026 to implement SB 79 locally "by 2030" (verified, LA City Planning SB 79 page). San Diego adopted Ordinance O-22099 phasing implementation with exemptions on 21 May 2026 (verified, city ordinance PDF). Santa Monica excludes 6 of its 7 transit zones until 2030 (source_lead, Westside Current).
- Metropolitan planning organizations must publish tiered stop maps with a rebuttable presumption of validity (verified, 65912.160(f)); SCAG published its methodology in July 2026 (source_lead, SCAG).
- First applications: San Mateo (near Caltrain, July 2026), Atherton (30 units, 6 affordable, filed 9 July 2026), Santa Monica (5 townhomes, August 2026) (source_lead, local press). No official permit counts exist yet. SB 79 projects should appear in housing element Annual Progress Reports for calendar year 2026, due April 2027 (source_lead, inferred).

**Rating: near.** On paper, SB 79's default standards are the closest match to the benchmark of any law reviewed: clear stop definitions, parcel-level height, density and FAR floors by tier, and a ministerial path. But the law lets the biggest cities (Los Angeles, San Francisco, San Diego, Santa Monica) exclude most station zones until about 2030 to 2031, which overlaps most of a 2027 to 2031 forecast window, and permits capacity-averaging alternative plans afterwards. Coverage is limited to 7 counties and the outer ring to cities of 35,000 or more people, and projects over 10 units carry a 7 to 13 percent affordability set-aside.

### 1.2 Predecessors and related California law

**SB 827 (2018), note only.** Would have exempted housing within 0.5 mile of a major transit stop or 0.25 mile of a high-quality transit corridor stop from local density, FAR and parking limits, with height floors of roughly 45 to 85 feet. It failed in the Senate Transportation and Housing Committee in April 2018 (source_lead, from general knowledge; not re-read). SB 50 (2019 to 2020) was a later failed version (source_lead).

**Statutory definitions (verified).**
- "Major transit stop", PRC 21064.3: a site containing an existing rail or bus rapid transit station, a ferry terminal served by bus or rail, or the intersection of 2 or more major bus routes with a service interval of 20 minutes or less in the morning and afternoon peaks (verified, PRC 21064.3, as amended by AB 2553, effective 1 January 2025). Before AB 2553 the bus interval was 15 minutes (source_lead).
- "High-quality transit corridor", PRC 21155(b): fixed-route bus service with service intervals no longer than 15 minutes during peak commute hours; for that section a major transit stop also includes stops in the regional transportation plan; a project counts as within 0.5 mile if no more than 25 percent of each parcel's area and no more than 10 percent of units (or 100 units, if fewer) lie beyond 0.5 mile (verified, PRC 21155(b)).

**State density bonus law, Gov. Code 65915 (verified, as amended by AB 87, effective 1 January 2026).**
- "Major transit stop" uses the PRC 21155(b) definition; "within one-half mile" means any point on the project is within 0.5 mile of any point on the stop's property, including transit-agency parking lots (verified, 65915(p) definitions).
- 100 percent affordable projects (up to 20 percent of units may be moderate income) within 0.5 mile of a major transit stop get no maximum density control and a height increase of up to 3 stories or 33 feet (verified, 65915(d)(2)(D) and (f)).
- Parking cap of 0.5 spaces per unit within 0.5 mile of a major transit stop with unobstructed access, for projects with at least 20 percent low-income or 11 percent very low income units (verified, 65915(p)(2)).

**AB 2011 (2022) and SB 6 (2022).** Neither is a transit law in its core, but AB 2011 (Gov. Code 65912.100 et seq.) sets higher standards on commercial corridors near transit: per the Urban Institute, at least 80 dwelling units per acre and 65 feet within 0.5 mile of a major transit stop after the AB 2243 (2024) expansion (source_lead, Urban Institute December 2025). SB 6 (Gov. Code 65852.24) allows housing on commercial land without a transit tie (source_lead). We did not re-read either statute.

### 1.3 AB 2097 (2022), parking, note only

Gov. Code 65863.2: a public agency may not impose or enforce any minimum automobile parking requirement on a project within 0.5 mile of a major transit stop as defined in PRC 21155 (verified, 65863.2(a), (e)(5)). A city may still require parking if it makes written findings within 30 days of a complete application of a substantially negative impact on its low-income housing need, special-needs housing or existing parking; that override is unavailable for housing projects with at least 20 percent affordable units or fewer than 20 units (verified, 65863.2(b), (c)).

## 2. Washington

### 2.1 HB 1491 (2025), transit-oriented housing development

**Citation and URL.** Third Substitute House Bill 1491, Chapter 267, Laws of 2025. Station-area rules codified at RCW 36.70A.840 (2025 c 267 s 3); definitions in RCW 36.70A.030 (s 2); parking in a new section of chapter 36.70A RCW (s 5); SEPA exemption in RCW 43.21C.229(5) (s 7). Session law: https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/biennium/2025-26/Pdf/Bills/Session%20Laws/House/1491-S3.SL.pdf ; RCW: https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=36.70A.840 (verified; RCW history shows no amendments after 2025 c 267).

**Dates.**
- Enacted: passed the House 22 April 2025 (57 yeas, 39 nays) and the Senate 15 April 2025 (30 yeas, 18 nays); approved by the Governor 13 May 2025 (verified, enrolled certificate).
- Effective: 27 July 2025 (verified, session law cover).
- Local compliance (verified, RCW 36.70A.840(15)): cities with comprehensive plan reviews due under RCW 36.70A.130(5)(a) (King, Pierce, Snohomish and Kitsap counties) must comply by the earlier of 31 December 2029 or their first implementation progress report after 31 December 2024. Other cities must comply within 6 months after their first comprehensive plan update due after 31 December 2024. MRSC translates the second group as 30 June 2026 for Whatcom and Clark counties and 30 June 2027 for Spokane County (source_lead, MRSC TOD page).
- State model ordinance: due by 30 June 2027 (verified, (16)(a)). Commerce lists a draft "TOD preemptive code" and a guidebook due winter 2026 (source_lead, Commerce TOD page).
- "Substantially similar" local plans: must be adopted by 30 June 2026 (verified, (18)(a)(i)). Vancouver filed a notice of intent in June 2026 (source_lead, Commerce TOD page).

**Station definitions (verified, RCW 36.70A.030 as amended by s 2).**
- "Rail station area": lots fully within an urban growth area and fully or partially within 0.5 mile walking distance of an entrance to a light rail station, a commuter rail stop in a city of more than 15,000 people, or a rail trolley stop west of the Cascades; or within 0.25 mile walking distance of a commuter rail stop in a city of 15,000 or fewer people (s 2(51)).
- "Bus station area": lots fully within an urban growth area and fully or partially within 0.25 mile walking distance of a stop designated as bus rapid transit in the agency's transit development plan, with a completed environmental determination and permanent high-capacity features such as elevated platforms, off-board fare collection, dedicated lanes or transit signal priority (s 2(52)).
- "Major transit stop" (used by HB 1110): stops on high capacity systems funded under chapter 81.104 RCW (excluding express-only bus), commuter rail stops, rail or fixed guideway stops, and BRT stops including those under construction (s 2(25)). No headway threshold is set in either definition.

**Density and FAR floors (verified, RCW 36.70A.840(2)).**
- Cities must allow residential and mixed-use development at "at least 3.5 floor area ratio, on average" in rail station areas and "at least 2.5 floor area ratio, on average" in bus station areas, or 3.0 on average if the city exempts up to 25 percent of bus station areas.
- Cities may not impose a maximum residential density in units per acre in station areas, and may not prohibit multifamily housing on any lot where any residential use is allowed (verified, (1), (2)(c)).
- Bonuses: an extra FAR of 1.5 for buildings that are entirely affordable or workforce housing for 50 years or permanent supportive housing (verified, (4)); floor area in units with 3 or more bedrooms does not count toward FAR (verified, (5)); greater height and density for all-mass-timber buildings in bus station areas (verified, (2)(a)(ii)(A)).
- FAR is measured on developable area, excluding critical areas and buffers (verified, RCW 36.70A.030(50)).
- No height floor is set. The "substantially similar" route requires a way to exceed 85 feet (verified, (18)(a)(i)(B)).

**By-right versus discretionary.** HB 1491 does not create a project-level entitlement; it requires cities to amend development regulations (verified, (10)). Cities may apply objective standards used for other multifamily housing in the same zone, including tree rules (verified, (10)(b)). Residential and mixed-use project actions in station areas are categorically exempt from SEPA review (verified, RCW 43.21C.229(5)). If a city misses its deadline, the Commerce model ordinance supersedes local rules until it complies (verified, (16)(b)).

**Opt-outs, alternative compliance and exemptions (verified unless noted).**
- Averaging: cities may set FAR above or below the floor in parts of a station area so long as the average maximum FAR of all residential and mixed-use areas meets the floor ((6)).
- Station area boundaries may be modified with Commerce approval ((2)(b)).
- Displacement extensions: Commerce "must" certify a 5-year extension for areas at high risk of displacement, renewable for further 5-year periods; during it the city may delay or set alternative FAR ((17)).
- Substantially similar plans adopted by 30 June 2026, exempt from appeal ((18)).
- Pre-2025 incentive programs requiring public benefits may continue if capacity is substantially similar ((9)).
- Lot exclusions: shoreline and critical areas, nonconforming lots, landmarks and historic districts created before the effective date, urban separators, certain industrial or agricultural lots, and tsunami inundation areas ((13)). Industrial and manufacturing areas need not change ((11)).
- Applies only to cities planning under the Growth Management Act, not to unincorporated urban growth areas.

**Affordability (verified, (7) and (8)).** Buildings constructed in a station area must keep for at least 50 years either 10 percent of units affordable (60 percent of AMI for rental under RCW 36.70A.030(5)), or 10 percent at 80 percent of AMI ("workforce housing") if at least 10 percent of units have more than 2 bedrooms, or 20 percent at 80 percent of AMI. Exemptions: lots already zoned at the TOD density before 1 January 2025, buildings under a stricter pre-31 December 2025 local requirement, and cities with a mandatory inclusionary program under RCW 36.70A.540. Cities must grant the 20-year multifamily property tax exemption to qualifying buildings ((8)). The Urban Institute flags that inclusionary rules this strict "may imperil construction" without compensation (source_lead, Urban Institute, December 2025).

**Parking (verified, s 5).** Cities may not require off-street parking for residential or mixed-use development in station areas, except disabled and short-term delivery spaces. Exceptions: a Commerce-certified safety study, or land within 1 mile of a commercial airport with at least 9,000,000 annual enplanements. Separately, SB 5184 (2025) removed parking minimums for many uses statewide (source_lead, Urban Institute).

**Parcel-level or average.** Station-area average FAR, not parcel-level. Multifamily must be allowed on every residential lot and density caps in units per acre are banned, which are parcel-level protections.

**Monitoring and data.** No production data yet; most deadlines have not passed. Commerce has issued a request for proposals for a programmatic environmental impact statement for TOD (funding period 6 November 2026 to 30 June 2027) (source_lead, Commerce RFP page). The infrastructure grant program (s 4) is "subject to appropriation" and was unfunded as of late 2025 (source_lead, Urban Institute).

**Rating: near.** HB 1491 defines stations precisely, sets FAR floors in the benchmark's own terms, eliminates parking and density caps, exempts projects from SEPA, and has a self-executing state fallback ordinance, which is the strongest enforcement design in this set. It falls short of "at" because FAR is an average across each station area, there is no height floor, the largest region has until 31 December 2029, displacement extensions are mandatory on application and renewable, and every new building faces a 10 to 20 percent, 50-year affordability mandate whose feasibility depends on the tax exemption. For a 2027 to 2031 forecast it is mostly a late-window effect in the Puget Sound region.

### 2.2 HB 1110 (2023), middle housing near transit, context

RCW 36.70A.635 (verified, current RCW text): cities of 25,000 to 75,000 people must allow at least 2 units per lot, or 4 units per lot within 0.25 mile walking distance of a major transit stop; cities of 75,000 or more must allow at least 4 units per lot, or 6 units per lot within 0.25 mile walking distance of a major transit stop. Cities may instead apply the rule to 75 percent of single-family lots, but the exempted 25 percent may not include areas within 0.5 mile walking distance of a major transit stop unless at higher displacement risk (verified, (4)). This is a unit-count rule, not a height or FAR rule.

## 3. Colorado HB24-1313 (2024), Housing in Transit-Oriented Communities

**Citation and URL.** House Bill 24-1313, Session Laws of 2024, Chapter 168 (source_lead, Urban Institute). The signed act adds a new Article 37 to Title 29 and numbers the transit sections 29-37-201 to 29-37-210 (verified, signed act, https://leg.colorado.gov/bill_files/47835/download). DOLA and later reports cite them as C.R.S. 29-35-201 to 29-35-210, so they were renumbered on codification (source_lead, DOLA). Section numbers below are the signed-act numbers.

**Dates (verified unless noted).**
- Enacted and effective: signed 13 May 2024, effective on signature under a safety clause (verified, bill page and signed act section 7).
- DOLA transit area maps: by 30 September 2024 (verified, 29-37-207(1)); published on time (source_lead, DOLA).
- Preliminary assessment report: by 30 June 2025 (verified, 29-37-204(3)).
- Affordability and anti-displacement strategies and the housing opportunity goal report: by 31 December 2026 (verified, 29-37-204(6) to (8)).
- Meet the goal: by 31 December 2027, after which DOLA issues a non-compliance notice (verified, 29-37-204(4), (8)(c)).
- Status reports every 3 years after the goal report; DOLA lists the first as due 31 December 2029 (verified for the 3-year rule, 29-37-204(9); date source_lead, DOLA).

**Station and corridor definitions (verified, 29-37-202, 29-37-207).**
- Transit station areas: within 0.5 mile of existing commuter bus rapid transit, commuter rail or light rail stations on routes in an applicable transit plan.
- Transit corridor areas: within 0.25 mile of urban BRT planned before 1 January 2030 in an MPO long-range plan or short-term transit plan, or bus routes with a planned or scheduled frequency of 15 minutes or better for 8 or more hours on weekdays.
- Optional transit areas: 0.25 mile from BRT planned for 2030 to 2050, routes at 30 minutes or better, and approved mixed-use neighborhoods.
- Subject jurisdictions ("transit-oriented communities"): local governments at least partly inside an MPO with 4,000 or more residents and at least 75 acres of transit area (verified, 29-37-202(11)). DOLA lists 32 jurisdictions covering 3,563,735 people, about 60 percent of the state population (source_lead, DOLA web page and JBC hearing response, November 2025).

**Density floor.** Each community's "housing opportunity goal" equals transit-area acreage, net of exempt parcels, multiplied by 40 dwelling units per acre (verified, 29-37-204(2)). The community meets it by designating "transit centers" whose total zoning capacity at least equals the goal (verified, 29-37-204(4)(b)). Each transit center must uniformly allow at least 15 dwelling units per acre of net housing density, and no parcel may be counted above 500 units per acre (verified, 29-37-205(1)(a)). Transit centers may lie in transit areas, optional transit areas, or within 0.25 mile of their boundaries (verified, 29-37-205(1)(e)); use of optional areas requires evidence that 40 units per acre is allowed "to the maximum extent feasible" on non-exempt parcels of 0.5 acre or more in the transit area (verified, 29-37-205(2)). There is no height or FAR floor; net density must reflect height, FAR, setback and parking rules, assuming surface parking (verified, 29-37-205(1)(b)).

**By-right versus discretionary.** Inside transit centers, multifamily development on parcels of 5 acres or less must go through an administrative approval process with no public hearing (verified, 29-37-205(1)(d), 29-37-103(2)). Larger parcels need only a target density. Communities keep discretionary processes for subdivisions, rezonings and variances, may approve projects below maximum density, and may deny or condition projects on infrastructure standards and objective traffic studies (verified, 29-37-203(3)).

**Opt-outs, alternative compliance and exemptions (verified).**
- Communities may allow "a very low amount of or no zoning capacity" in one transit area while concentrating it in another (29-37-203(3)(d)).
- A long exempt-parcel list as of 1 January 2024: no public water and sewer, agricultural, open space or industrial zoning, floodways and 100-year floodplains, utility easements, cemeteries, conservation easements, airports, rights-of-way, mobile home parks, government- and school-owned land, parks, and parcels cut off from the station by a limited-access highway or railroad beyond a 0.5 mile walkshed, plus parcels the state approves as undevelopable (29-37-202(2)).
- Water-supply notice may reduce the goal for 3-year periods (29-37-204(5)).
- No corridor areas in communities with 20 percent or more of their area in manufactured home zoning (29-37-207(3)(c)).
- Enforcement: the only stated consequence is a non-compliance notice and loss of "certified" status, which gates the $35 million infrastructure grant fund and the transit-oriented affordable housing tax credit (verified, 29-37-204(8)(c), (d)). We found no attorney general or court enforcement path in the signed act.

**Affordability.** No unit set-aside. Each community must adopt at least 2 strategies from a standard affordability menu (inclusionary zoning, public land, fee waivers, density bonus, expedited review, family-sized units and others), at least 1 long-term strategy (dedicated revenue, short-term rental regulation, land trusts and others), and 2 anti-displacement strategies (verified, 29-37-204(6), (7); 29-37-208).

**Parking.** Not in HB24-1313. Companion law HB24-1304 removed minimum parking for residential and mixed-use projects in metropolitan transit service areas, with local exceptions for affordable or large projects on substantial evidence (source_lead, Urban Institute; not read).

**Parcel-level or average.** Jurisdiction-wide aggregate zoning capacity. This is the clearest example of the "on average" design the owner asked us to flag.

**Monitoring and data.** As of 3 October 2025, 19 of 32 communities had compliant preliminary reports, 3 were in progress and 10 were not compliant (source_lead, Colorado Politics, 7 October 2025, citing DOLA). By 4 December 2025, 22 of 32 had filed preliminary reports (source_lead, DOLA second annual report to the legislature). DOLA had spent $543,827 and encumbered $85,878 of the $35,000,000 grant allocation by 25 November 2025 (source_lead, JBC hearing response). DOLA has said "This law is about Zoning Capacity in transit-oriented areas, not housing unit production" (source_lead, DOLA web page). DOLA keeps a quarterly compliance dashboard (source_lead).

**Rating: weaker.** HB24-1313 sets a jurisdiction-wide zoning capacity target, not parcel-level height or FAR rights at stations. Localities choose where transit centers go, may leave some station areas with little or no new capacity, need only allow 15 units per acre in any given center, and face no penalty beyond losing grant and tax credit eligibility. It is a planning-and-incentive law and should not be used as an analogue for the effect of a direct upzoning.

## 4. Oregon climate-friendly areas, OAR 660-012-0310 to 660-012-0320

**Citation and URL.** Oregon Administrative Rules, chapter 660, division 12, rules 0310 (applicability and location), 0315 (designation and capacity) and 0320 (land use requirements), adopted by the Land Conservation and Development Commission under the Climate-Friendly and Equitable Communities (CFEC) program. Official text: https://secure.sos.state.or.us/oard/displayDivisionRules.action?selectedDivision=3062 (verified). History: temporary adoption 1 June 2022, permanent 17 August 2022, amended 7 November 2023 (verified, rule history notes).

**Dates (verified, OAR 660-012-0012 unless noted).**
- Rules effective 17 August 2022.
- Climate-friendly area study due 31 December 2023 (not extendable).
- Land use requirements and plan element due 31 December 2024, unless the DLCD director approves alternative dates; alternative dates must complete most elements by 31 December 2029.
- Eugene's adoption deadline is December 2026, with hearings in November 2026 (source_lead, City of Eugene). Albany adopted 6 overlay zones effective 23 May 2025 (source_lead, City of Albany).

**Scope and definition (verified, 0310).** Applies to cities and counties in metropolitan areas outside Portland Metro with more than 5,000 people in an urban growth boundary. Portland Metro instead designates Metro 2040 regional and town centers (verified, 0012(4)(d)). There is no "major transit stop" definition: areas must be in existing or planned urban centers, downtowns or "transit-served corridors", "served, or planned for service, by high quality pedestrian, bicycle, and transit services", with a minimum width of 750 feet (verified, 0310(2)). The program covers the Albany, Bend, Corvallis, Eugene-Springfield, Grants Pass, Medford, Portland and Salem-Keizer metro areas (source_lead, DLCD CFEC page).

**Size.** Cities over 10,000 people must designate areas able to hold at least 30 percent of total current and future housing units, using standard assumptions (85 feet where no height limit, 30 percent of building capacity residential, 900 square feet per unit); cities of 10,000 or fewer designate at least 25 acres (verified, 0315(1) to (3)).

**Density and height floors (verified, 0320(8)).**

| City population | Minimum residential density (dwelling units per net acre) | Maximum height may not be set below | Where it must apply |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5,001 to 25,000 | 15 | 50 feet | All climate-friendly areas |
| 25,001 to 50,000 | 20 | 60 feet | At least one area of 25 acres or more |
| Over 50,000 | 25 | 85 feet | At least one area of 25 acres or more |

Alternative: findings showing zoned building capacity of at least 60,000, 90,000 or 120,000 square feet per net acre for the same three city sizes, with a minimum of 15 units per acre or FAR 1.0 (verified, 0320(9)). Minimum densities do not apply to mixed-use buildings at FAR 2.0 or more (verified, 0320(8)). Maximum density limits are banned (verified, 0320(6)).

**By-right versus discretionary.** Multi-unit housing, offices, non-auto retail and public uses must be "outright permitted" in the areas (verified, 0320(2)).

**Opt-outs and alternative compliance.** Local choice of location; alternative capacity methodology (verified, 0320(10)); alternative development standards (verified, 0320(9)); alternative compliance dates (verified, 0012(3)); director exemptions for jurisdictions under 10,000 people (source_lead, DLCD implementation guide). Enforcement is through periodic review or an enforcement order (verified, 0310(5)).

**Affordability.** No set-aside; local governments must list housing production and anti-displacement strategies (verified, 0315(6)(d)).

**Parking (verified).** Metro-area cities may not enforce parking mandates on lots within 0.75 mile of rail stops or 0.5 mile of frequent transit corridors (at least 4 arrivals per hour at peak), effective for applications after 31 December 2022 (OAR 660-012-0440; 0012(5)(e)). In climate-friendly areas, cities must either remove mandates or adopt parking management (OAR 660-012-0435).

**Parcel-level or average.** Area-level: the floors apply inside designated areas, and the larger floors need cover only one 25-acre area.

**Monitoring and data.** DLCD posts study reports and alternative date approvals (source_lead). We found no production data for climate-friendly areas.

**Rating: weaker.** The climate-friendly area rules do set real height and density floors and ban maximum densities, but the areas are locally chosen urban centers, not station areas around defined transit stops, and there is no distance threshold. The highest floors (85 feet, 25 units per acre) bind only one 25-acre area per large city, and adoption deadlines have slid from 2024 to 2026 or later in several metros. Oregon's parking rules near transit, by contrast, are among the strongest anywhere.

## 5. British Columbia Bill 47 (2023), transit-oriented areas (international comparator)

**In scope?** Yes, as a comparator. It is the purest example of province-prescribed, parcel-level FAR and height floors by distance from named stations, which is what the benchmark describes. Its effects should not be pooled with US laws for estimating permits, because Canadian housing finance and approval systems differ.

**Citation and URL.** Housing Statutes (Transit-Oriented Areas) Amendment Act, 2023, S.B.C. 2023, c. 48, adding Local Government Act s. 481.01 and s. 525.1 (and parallel Vancouver Charter provisions) (verified, https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/hstats/hstats/382825264). Local Government Transit-Oriented Areas Regulation, B.C. Reg. 263/2023 (verified, https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/263_2023). Provincial Policy Manual: Transit-Oriented Areas (verified, official provincial PDF).

**Dates.** Royal assent 30 November 2023 (source_lead). Municipalities had to designate TOAs by bylaw and update parking bylaws by 30 June 2024 (verified for parking start date, Policy Manual; designation deadline source_lead). Zoning in TOAs must be consistent with official community plans after 31 December 2025 (verified, Policy Manual).

**Station definition.** A "transit station" is a prescribed (listed) bus stop, bus exchange, passenger rail station or other facility, including planned ones (verified, LGA s. 455). The regulation lists the stations by coordinates. TOA radius is 800 m for passenger rail (SkyTrain) and 400 m for bus exchanges and West Coast Express (commuter rail) stations; a parcel cut by the boundary is wholly inside (verified, Reg. s. 2).

**Floors (verified, Reg. s. 5; tiers 1 to 3 are rail, 4 and 5 are bus or West Coast Express).**

| Tier | Distance | Metro Vancouver SkyTrain municipalities | Metro bus or West Coast Express municipalities | Larger regional cities (for example Kelowna, Victoria) | Smallest listed (Lantzville, Penticton, Whistler) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 200 m or less from rail | FAR 5.0, 20 storeys | | | |
| 2 | 200 to 400 m from rail | FAR 4.0, 12 storeys | | | |
| 3 | 400 to 800 m from rail | FAR 3.0, 8 storeys | | | |
| 4 | 200 m or less from bus | | FAR 4.0, 12 storeys | FAR 3.5, 10 storeys | FAR 2.5, 6 storeys |
| 5 | 200 to 400 m from bus | | FAR 3.0, 8 storeys | FAR 2.5, 6 storeys | FAR 1.5, 4 storeys |

**By-right versus discretionary.** A local government "must not exercise the powers [to zone] to prohibit or restrict a density of use, or a size or dimension of buildings" set in the regulation on land in a TOA zoned for residential use (verified, LGA s. 481.01). In practice this is not pre-zoning: per the Province, an owner still applies to rezone, but the council may not refuse "based on height and density alone" if the proposal is at or below the floor (verified, Policy Manual). Other grounds for refusal remain (source_lead, Storeys, December 2023, cited by Urban Institute).

**Opt-outs and exemptions.** Exemptions may be set by regulation (verified, s. 481.01). Parcels where residential use is ancillary to agricultural or industrial use are exempt (verified, Policy Manual). Some municipalities received deferrals for infrastructure constraints (source_lead, not verified).

**Affordability.** None in the Act (source_lead, Urban Institute table 3 note).

**Parking.** Local governments must not require off-street residential parking in a TOA, other than disabled spaces (verified, LGA s. 525.1).

**Parcel-level or average.** Parcel-level.

**Monitoring and data.** We found no provincial TOA production report. BC publishes housing target progress reports for selected municipalities, which could be mined later (source_lead).

**Rating: near.** Bill 47 is the cleanest statement of the benchmark: provincially listed stations, three distance tiers, parcel-level FAR and storey floors, and no parking minimums. It falls short of "at" because the floors are a ceiling on refusals rather than a zoning right, so each project still needs a rezoning, and the bus tiers outside Metro Vancouver are modest (FAR 1.5 to 3.5).

## 6. Existing reports that classify state TOD laws

None of the reports we found assigns strong or weak ratings to named laws. Two compare design features, which is the closest thing to a taxonomy.

1. **Urban Institute, "Mandating Density Near Transit" (Freemark, Lo and Su, December 2025)** (verified, read in full via a copy on leg.wa.gov). Compares 7 laws (BC Bill 47, California AB 2011 and SB 79, Colorado HB24-1313, Massachusetts MBTA Communities, Ontario Provincial Planning Statement 2024, Washington HB 1491) on transit types, distance tiers, density metric (FAR, units per acre, height, people and jobs per acre) and minimum versus average rules. It gives no grades, but it explicitly warns that average-density mandates (Colorado, Washington, and SB 79 alternative plans) "may create openings for cities seeking to evade the intent" by putting density where redevelopment is unlikely, and that strict inclusionary rules "may imperil construction". Its recommendations: minimum (not average) density rules, fines for non-compliance, compensation for affordability mandates, infrastructure grants, public land, transit agency development powers, and funded tracking. Caution: its SB 79 table shows 2.5 FAR and 60 units per acre for the outer Tier 2 ring, while the statute says 2.5 FAR and 80 units per acre (65912.157(a)(6)); it also folds the adjacency bonus into a 200-foot row. Use the statute.
2. **ACEEE, "Five Key Components to Include in State Transit-Oriented Development Laws" (Christi Nakajima, 31 October 2025)** (verified). A best-practice blog drawing on SB 79, HB 1491, HB24-1313, AB 2097 and AB 2011. Components: easy compliance with flexibility (it praises averaging in Washington and Colorado and Washington's fallback ordinance; it criticizes Massachusetts' complex guidelines), mixed use, affordability requirements or incentives, lower costs and timelines (parking removal, by-right review), and funding plus technical assistance. No ratings. Note that ACEEE treats averaging as a virtue, the opposite of the Urban Institute and of our "poison pill" framing.
3. **Metropolitan Abundance Project, "Build More Homes Near Transit Act" model bill** (verified, metroabundance.org). Not a rating, but it is an implicit benchmark: allowed use within 0.5 mile, ministerial approval with a 30-day deemed-approved clock, Tier 1 floors of 75 feet, FAR 3.5 and 120 units per acre and Tier 2 floors of 55 feet, FAR 2.5 and 80 units per acre (mirroring SB 79), no parking mandates, and capacity-equivalent local plans that cannot rely on unlikely-to-develop sites or cut any site by more than 50 percent. It says it combines SB 79's direct rights, HB 1491's fallback and HB24-1313's certification. Its state profiles narrate lessons but do not grade.
4. **University of Washington, "Finding Common Ground: Best Practices for Policies Supporting Transit-Oriented Development" (Washington Center for Real Estate Research and Mobility Innovation Center, January 2024)** (source_lead, read only search excerpts). Reviews California and other state policies to inform Washington; recommends tiered FAR minimums of 1.5 to 5, clear timelines and enforceable consequences, and endorses state "average minimum FAR" with local parcel-level detail. No ratings found in the excerpts. A related Joint Transportation Committee study, "Recommendations for transit oriented development policies in Washington state", Phases I and II (June and December 2025), exists (source_lead).
5. **NCSL (2012) TOD state policy overview.** Not re-read in this package. To our knowledge it is a descriptive inventory of state TOD programs and incentives, without ratings (source_lead).

Implication: no published scheme rates TOD laws the way Mercatus rates ADU laws, so our "at / near / weaker" classification would be original. The Urban Institute's minimum-versus-average distinction and MAP's anti-evasion rules give citable support for treating averaging, exclusion maps and delays as weakening features.

## 7. Notes for the Wisconsin design

- Every law reviewed uses 0.5 mile (about 800 m) as the outer radius for rail, and 0.25 mile (about 400 m) for bus or BRT in Washington, Colorado and BC. California's major transit stop definition was loosened to 20-minute bus headways in 2025, but SB 79 itself counts buses only where they run in dedicated lanes.
- Frequency thresholds, where stated: California commuter rail at 48 or 72 trains per day; Colorado bus at 15 minutes or better for 8 or more weekday hours; California major transit stop bus intersections at 20 minutes in peaks; Oregon parking rule at 4 or more arrivals per hour in peaks. Washington and BC use mode and named-station lists rather than headways.
- Under a Washington-style definition (BRT with fixed assets, rail and streetcar), Milwaukee's Connect 1 BRT, The Hop streetcar and the Amtrak Hiawatha stations, and Madison's Rapid Route A, would plausibly qualify (source_lead, not checked against service data).
